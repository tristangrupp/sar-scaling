SAR Bench: sharpening and despeckling radar images

Six experiments test whether open radar data can improve Sentinel-1 images. The comparisons cover X-band imagery, Sentinel-1 Stripmap, NISAR bandwidth pairs and learned speckle filters.

Main result

X-band data did not improve the final Sentinel-1 model under the settings tested. It did not help as a direct image target or an edge loss. Pretraining helped some short runs, but the advantage disappeared with the full training method and budget. Same-sensor training and learned despeckling produced the clearest gains.

These results come from small research datasets. They do not establish a general limit on radar super-resolution.

Experiments 0–5: complete

0
Where the data overlaps

The atlas found 1,069 candidate Umbra–Stripmap pairs at 12 sites, 211 ICEYE pairs at 7 sites and 110 Capella pairs at 4 sites. Most Capella imagery uses a different polarization from Sentinel-1 IW over land.

1
Predicting X-band images from Sentinel-1

The tested models mainly smoothed the input. Detail correlation was 0.04, versus 0.03 for bicubic interpolation. The repeat-image check shows how alignment and viewing geometry also limit this score.

2
Training Sentinel-1 IW against Stripmap

The same-sensor model beat bicubic, largely through despeckling. Adding X-band edge loss reduced held-out SSIM by 0.21 points. More inland data and a change mask made little difference.

3
Training with NISAR bandwidth pairs

Images from the same acquisition provide a cleaner test of the method. The tuned model beat bicubic on all five metrics in the distant test regions. X-band edge loss did not help; GAN fine-tuning added texture while reducing detail correlation. These results apply to L-band NISAR.

4
Reducing Sentinel-1 speckle

A VV+VH Noise2Noise model gained about 0.7 dB PSNR over Lee. WorldCover classification accuracy rose from 0.770 for raw VV+VH to 0.806. Field-boundary detection did not improve, and textured desert remained difficult.

5
Pretraining across radar bands

An X-band-only model beat bicubic against Stripmap references, and pretraining helped short runs. With 200 real training tiles and 8,000 steps, none of 40 comparisons met the rule for an improvement over training from scratch.

How to read the comparisons

Sentinel-1 IW uses a 10 m pixel grid, with spatial resolution of about 20 m. Drawing an output on a finer grid does not establish that it resolves finer features. We check whether added detail agrees with radar references, then test whether cleaner images help mapping.

The references contain speckle and can differ in wavelength, date, viewing angle and alignment. Each page reports those limits. Experiments 2 and 5 adapt a published method, but their absolute scores cannot be compared directly with the paper’s whole-scene results.

Terms used on these pages

SAR and speckle
Synthetic aperture radar images reflected microwave signals. Speckle is the grainy variation caused by interference between returns.
X-, C- and L-band
Radar wavelength groups: about 3 cm, 5.6 cm and 24 cm here. They can show different scattering patterns over the same ground.
IW and Stripmap
Sentinel-1 acquisition modes. IW covers wide areas; Stripmap SLC provides the finer reference used in this study.
VV, VH and HH
Transmit/receive polarization channels. Comparisons across channels require care because the image contrast can differ.
Bicubic
A standard interpolation baseline. It makes a finer pixel grid without learning new detail.
Held out, seed and zero-shot
Held-out data is excluded from training. A seed controls training randomness. Zero-shot means applying a trained model to a new domain without retraining.
SSIM, PSNR and MAE
Measures of structural similarity, signal-to-error ratio and average absolute error. Higher SSIM and PSNR are better; lower MAE is better. Read scores within each experiment’s evaluation setup.
Detail correlation and energy
Correlation checks whether fine detail agrees with the reference. Energy measures its strength. More energy alone can mean more noise or invented texture.
Edge distance and ENL
Edge distances measure how far one image’s edges lie from another’s. ENL (equivalent number of looks) measures smoothness in uniform areas; high values can also reflect excessive filtering.
CNN, GAN and Noise2Noise
A CNN learns image filters. A GAN adds a discriminator to encourage realistic-looking output. Noise2Noise learns from noisy image pairs rather than a clean target.
